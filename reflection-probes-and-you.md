# Reflection probes and You

# Introduction

Reflection probes are an essential part of PBR (Physics Based Rendering) materials and lighting systems. That is why when there are no manual probes, there are automated reflection probes that get dotted around. The problem is, with a dynamic world like Second Life, it is extremely difficult and in some cases even impossible to write an algorithm that can place the probes automatically and correctly. That is why manual probes are needed.

# What even are reflection probes?

To put it simply, a reflection probe is a 360 degree photo that can be used to display approximate reflections and add highlights or some ambient light. That photo is later sampled from when rendering the scene. While the reflection data provided by it is quite blurry and not the most accurate, it is very fast in terms of performance, and does a good enough job in most cases. Reflection probes and various variants of them are and have been widely used in many different video games over the last decade and more, with more recent games having more of them, and some being even more dynamic.

It is also worth noting, that reflection probes themselves are a cheap, but good enough approximation of reflections, to have accurate reflections you’d need to use raytracing, and even then, blurry reflections would require a lot of rays to sample accurately, therefore even raytraced games would use a mix of both.

You might have seen some so-called ‘environment boxes’ or ‘shine boxes’ that use projector lights to shine a picture onto your shiny clothes, whether they have a picture of an italian village, photo studio or just some coloured patterns, what they are actually trying to achieve is what the reflection probes do. Effectively reflection probes are the proper way of doing what those ‘boxes’ were trying to mimic.

**Note**: Reflection probes belong in the environment, not as an attachment, they also do not work as part of physics enabled objects such as cars. If they do work in your viewer that is due to a bug, and it will be fixed in the future, so you cannot rely on that behaviour.\
See: <https://wiki.secondlife.com/wiki/PBR_Materials#Unsupported_use-cases>


## When do I need a reflection probe?

Simple answer - always… well… not exactly, for large outdoor areas, the automatically placed probes will do a good enough job, so while you may not need to place them yourself, you still need the probes.

However, as mentioned before, automated probes don’t do the job well when dealing with interiors, so you’ll likely to end up seeing reflections of the sky on things that are indoors, or you might have seen that bunch of stuff looks blue with PBR viewer - that is why, it is likely that the reflection probe was auto placed outside the wall or ceiling and is reflecting the sky onto interior objects. This is the primary case where you’ll want to have a manual probe.

Warning: auto-placed probes are generated by the viewer, while generally the same version of the viewer will use the same code to do it (different Third Party Viewers could also tweak their algorithms), it might not be fully deterministic, so two people in the same area might get different results, and reflections might not look exactly the same for different people. This might depend on how fast the area rezzed, or which objects rezzed first etc. which in turn can vary on each teleport, on which objects are still in the cache and so on.\
This means that in some cases, where you want perfect control over the reflections, even in outdoor scenes, you might want to use manually placed probes.


## So, where do I get one?

First of all - you **do not** need to buy reflection probes. Anyone can make one, and it is just a property of any object. Just like lights. Just rezz a box, go into the feature tab and you’re all set. But we’ll get into that a bit later.

### Differences between box and sphere probes

First and most obvious difference between the two is the influence area and shape, since they only affect objects inside them and based on the surroundings one might fit in better than the other. If you have a round room, the sphere will do great, but a lot of rooms are cuboid shaped.

Another difference is their edge fading - boxes are sharp, and spheres are quite feathered:

Making spheres excelend for large outdoor areas, or filling in rooms when you do not have any other rooms on the other side of the walls, like in various skyboxes. This would allow you to have more, but smaller probes with smooth blending, which allows for more accurate reflections, as the reflected image is based on the centre of the reflection probe, meaning that the further away from the centre the shiny object is, the less and less accurate the reflection. (It is quite tricky to show this in pictures, so it is something for you to experiment with)

#### Default Value

Ambience of 1.0 is considered to be the default value because glTF specification does not define ambient lights and implementations are expected to use techniques such as Image-based lighting to provide more realistic lighting. And in SL that would be the reflection probes. However, for legacy reasons and for providing mood and fantasy lighting, where you might want to tint everything a specific colour and such, this value can be changed to suit your needs. And since PBR is ‘Physically’ based, real life physics do not have ambient light either - global illumination is the real life equivalent, which to put simply is all the light that bounces around your room from all the walls, ceilings and any other objects. While true global illumination is an extremely complex effect (for true-to-life results you’d need ray tracing with thousands of rays), reflection probes are a few steps up from ambient light, in terms of a better approximation with a lot less complexity than full GI.


### Static vs Dynamic probe mode

This one is quite simple and it depends on your ‘Reflection Detail’ setting in your graphics options. If graphic settings are set to ‘Realtime’ then reflection probes that are set to dynamic will also render avatars, which are normally skipped. This setting also makes the probe render a lot more often, which has a sizable impact on your framerate. With the planar mirror update being released, I can’t think of any reason to have dynamic probes as mirrors have that setting, but their dynamic part is controlled by ‘Mirrors’ checkbox in the graphic setting and the Mirror (Everything) mode in probe type.

# Mirrors and Reflection Probes

Based on how up to date your viewer is, you might have access to ‘Mirror’ type reflection probes, sometimes also called ‘Planar Mirrors’ or ‘Planar Reflection Probes’, to differentiate them from regular reflection probes, which are - spherical or cuboid. Standard reflection probes **should not** be used as mirrors, while both types are reflection probes, they serve a different purpose.

**Beware**: some people did use dynamic reflection probes as “mirrors” before the update, and mislabeled them as if they were actual mirrors. See [Mirror type probe vs Regular probe](#mirror-type-probe-vs-regular-probe) section below.


## How do I set up a mirror?

Making a mirror is very simple, but a few extra steps compared to a regular probe. Before we begin, make sure Mirrors are enabled in your graphics settings.

1. Start with rezzing two boxes.

2. Set PBR material on one of the boxes, the same way as in the Preparation step of the guide - Metallic 1.0, Roughness 0.0, with white base colour.

   1. If you already have a shiny surface that will be your mirror object (Like a shiny floor or a wall, or an actual mirror mesh) - double check that it does have correct material settings on the face that will be acting as a mirror, but hopefully it will already have good enough values to be reflective. And you can assume that ‘shiny box’ in further steps just means your mirror object.

3. Select the top face of the second box, and change the colour of it to something else. PBR or Blinn-Phong doesn’t matter. We just want an easy way to tell which face is the top one as the mirror probe requires specific orientation.

4. Now, resize the shiny box to the size you want your mirror surface to be.

   1. Optional if you have an object mentioned in 2.a.

5. **Rotate** and position the other box - the soon to be mirror probe - in front of your shiny box, in such a way that the centre of it is on the surface of the shiny object, (it might be slightly in front or inside) and the top face is looking towards us - or ‘out of the mirror’. The top face being a different colour in the previous step should make this quite simple. If your mirror surface is at an angle, you’ll have to match that angle as closely as possible.

6. Now, we need to resize it a little - make it a lot thinner (doesn’t have to be paper thin, but around 0.04-0.06 is pretty decent), and then a bit taller and wider than our shiny box

7. Go into Features tab and enable Reflection Probe, Pick Box, and Mirror (Everything) in the options

8. We’re nearly done!

9. Adjust the size of the probe a little, so the weird stretchy lines on the sides disappear by making the probe wider/taller, as well as adjusting its position so the centre of the probe is very close to the surface of our shiny box.. Slight adjustments closer/further from the surface should help dial in the best result.


### Some of the most common issues

- Don’t forget to rotate them the right way, if the probe remains facing up, you’ll see a horizontal line dividing the reflection, and only the top half is going to work like a mirror. That’s why I recommend colouring the top face. Example of bad mirror orientation:

- Reflection looks like a long tunnel with a small square in the middle acting like a mirror - that’s likely because the centre of the probe is a bit too far from the surface of the mirror object.

- If the centre of the mirror probe is too far away from the surface, the reflection will look dim:

- If the centre of the mirror probe is too far inside the shiny object, it will use a standard-style reflection and not mirror kind. A good way to determine where the centre is, is to look at the movement axis when in move mode, the lines should be just in front of the surface.

- Gap in the floor appearing in front of the mirror is likely to be caused by the mirror probe being too thick (if the mirror is too thin, and it is too deep in, you won’t see a reflection at all), and the centre of it being to far away from the shiny surface:

### Mirror limitations

- Only a single mirror will be active at the time - the one nearest to your camera. Mirrors are very performance intensive and likely to cut your framerate in half - because it needs to render the whole scene twice, once from your view and once from the mirror’s view, that’s why this limit is needed.

  - Mirrors that are in the view, but are not the one closest to the camera will revert to reflections similar to standard reflection probes.

  - Reducing mirror resolution and update rate in the graphics options will improve the framerate at a cost of visual quality.

  - Side note: shadows have similar-type-of performance impact, as they also re-render the whole scene from the light’s point of view, however, shadows render a “black and white” (technically it is something else, I’m just keeping it simple) image without any textures and lower resolution than mirrors so the actual frame rate impact is a lot smaller.

- Since only one mirror is active at the time, mirrors can’t really reflect other mirrors, they will show reflections from regular or inactive mirrors, but due to warped projection of inactive mirrors and regular probes, infinity mirror effect will be very heavily distorted, and might not even work a lot of the time.

- There might be some weird artifacting if the mirror surface is touching the floor.

# Tips and Tricks

- Link your box probes and box lights together and then go into the Features tab and set physics to None - linking 2 probes make them cost 1 Land Impact, giving you two probes for 1 Land Impact. Spheres or other shapes have different LI weights, and may need more of them before any savings are realised.

  - It must be the physics shape option in the features tab, phantom does not affect LI.

  - If linking to other parts that do need physics, edit linked, and only disable physics on the probes, as you might want to keep physics of the other objects.

  - This trick works because different prims, and meshes might have non-integer impacts on various metrics, and the linkset’s total cost is rounded as a whole, after all different weights are summed up, so if two objects on their own are 0.5 LI rounded up they end up as 1 LI each, but when linked together 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 and it stays at 1 when rounded.

  - Once you set up a sphere probe, you can go into the object tab and change the Pirm type to box, keeping the reflection probe as sphere, this will enable the LI savings as a box is cheaper than a sphere.

- Regular (non-mirror) Static reflection probes update quite rarely and are quite low resolution, also the viewer only uses the nearest 256 reflection probes. Therefore their performance impact is very small, so feel free to use as many of them as you need.

  - Dynamic probes and Mirrors update a lot more often, so use them sparingly. Linden Lab referred to them as ‘Hero’ probes for a reason, they have big visual impact, but also big performance impact too.

- And of course PBR materials will look better than equivalent Blinn-Phong/Legacy materials in correct lighting conditions, so here’s a pretty good free and full-perm material pack to get you started: <https://marketplace.secondlife.com/p/AmbientCG-PBR-Material-Megapack/25595913>

## Tips from Zanibar of Alchemy viewer team:

- In large or outdoor scenes, use large sphere probes. The goal isn't accurate lighting and reflections, it's 'good enough'

- Sphere probes, in general, just look WAY better than box probes. They also blend beautifully

- Box probes can be rotated to adjust their influence!

- The only settings a probe cares about are its probe settings and its rotation, position, and scale. All other prim params are ignored.

- You can probably get away with using sphere probes in a skybox. Not only will it make blending between rooms easier, but it also lets you handle irregular rooms better.

- Manual Probes ALWAYS override auto probes

- The EEP's 'Reflection Probe Ambience' is the minimum probe ambience for all probes; you can make their ambience even more intense with individual ambience settings on the probes. This is useful for difficult interior scenes, just keep in mind you might need some extra blending help.

- You can sometimes ease the blending between rooms with a sphere probe, it's a bit tricky though

- Make sure your build's walls are thick so that you can afford to do a lot of overlap and blending

- It's ok for the probes to not be perfect. They shouldn't be perfect. The ultimate goal of a probe is to give you a general reflection and lighting map for PBR objects to work with.
